> $6.3bn left Binance on 29 October. It's the biggest crypto theft on record.
>
> The FBI has attributed it to North Korea. Blockchain analytics firms are tracing the coins, and about half has been frozen.
>
> But stolen crypto is only useful once it leaves the blockchain. And the moment it leaves, the trail stops being wallets and starts being companies.
>
> This heist even started with one. Binance says the attacker posed as a project director at a company that doesn't exist, ran fake interviews, and sent an engineer a "coding test" that infected his laptop. From there, they worked their way to the wallets.
>
> Billions are still in the attackers' hands. To spend them, they have to become ordinary money. A 2023 US Treasury and DOJ case shows how earlier North Korean thefts made that trip:
>
> 1) The stolen crypto was sold off-exchange to OTC traders in China.
> 2) The traders paid out in regular currency.
> 3) A Hong Kong-based trader routed those payments through front companies, which helped North Korean actors get past banks' anti-money-laundering checks.
> 4) A North Korean banker had the front companies buy goods for North Korea.
> 5) To the supplier's bank, it looked like a trading company paying an invoice. Nothing about it said crypto.
>
> --
>
> So a bank that never touches a crypto wallet can still end up processing the cash-out.
>
> --
>
> The questions for risk teams are:
> -Who owns the company sending this wire?
> -Who else shares its directors, its address, or the person who registered it?
>
> Blockchain analytics follow the coins to the edge of the chain. Sayari picks up the trail from there: who owns and runs the companies on the other side, and what they're connected to, across 250+ jurisdictions. So the trading firm on the wire and the network behind it show up in one place.
