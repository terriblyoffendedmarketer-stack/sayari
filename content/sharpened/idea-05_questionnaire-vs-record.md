# Idea 5, sharpened: Questionnaire vs record (post 3 pitch)

**Core truth:** a supplier's self-filled form and an official ownership record can say opposite things. Only the record is evidence. Often the supplier isn't lying; they don't know their own ownership chain.

## 1. What the idea is
From the idea doc (tab "5"), picked by Ekshika. Before working with a supplier, companies send a **questionnaire**: "Is any owner of your company on a sanctions list? Yes / No." The supplier ticks "No" and signs. Many teams file that and move on, because checking every owner themselves is slow.
The post puts two "documents" side by side: **left**, the form ticked "No"; **right**, the official registry record of the same (invented) company showing **two owners at 25% each, both sanctioned**. Under US rules, owners who together hold 50% or more make the company itself sanctioned, even though neither owner is a majority and the company's name is on no list.

## 2. Why they'd relate
Everyone in compliance or third-party risk sends and chases these forms. They know the feeling: *"the form says fine, my gut says I haven't actually checked."* It's the gap between "we did the process" and "we know the answer", and their name is on the file either way.

## 3. Is it real?
Strong evidence:
- Sayari case studies: **tier-1 questionnaire response rates "well below 50%"**, and "even cooperative suppliers typically lacked visibility into their own upstream supply chains" (top-5 grocery retailer). "Self-attestation cannot reveal foreign government ties hidden in ownership chains" (#3 US telecom). Questionnaires "break down beyond tier one" (top-3 tech company). A European auto group ran forced-labor checks on "manual processes and supplier self-attestation only".
- The rule is real: OFAC's 50% rule counts owners **in aggregate** (OFAC FAQ 401: two blocked owners at 25% each = a blocked company).
- Enforcement shows the flip side: in PURE (OFAC, $466,200) a questionnaire *did* name the sanctioned owner, but it never reached the screening. Forms are weak evidence either way.

## 5. Product fit
Strong and honest. Sayari builds ownership from **official registry records, with the source attached** ("who owns the owner"), so an analyst can check the form against the record instead of trusting it. The telecom's Head of TPRM said it outright: *"We can now independently verify vendor ownership claims against authoritative source data, rather than relying on vendor self-reporting."*
Possible ending: "Sayari reads the registry, not the form: the owners, their owners, and the record behind each one."
Limit: Sayari doesn't replace questionnaires; it checks them. Don't claim otherwise.

## 6. Hooks
- **A. "The supplier ticked 'No.' The registry didn't."** The plain contrast. Lands because every reader has filed a ticked form; it's instantly recognizable.
- **B. "Both are 'documents.' Only one answers 'how did you know?'"** The auditor moment. Lands on the quiet audit fear, and leads straight to "source, not score".
- **C. "25% + 25% = a sanctioned company. The form can't do that maths."** The surprising rule. Lands as a "wait, really?" learning moment, the most saveable, but it needs the rule explained in one line.
- **D. "Your supplier isn't lying. They just don't know who owns them either."** The empathetic twist (from the grocery case). Lands because it takes the blame off everyone and names the real gap.
**My pick: D as the hook, C as the twist inside the caption, B as the closing line.** D is the most human and least accusatory (true to "the enemy is the gap, not people"). C gives the save-worthy lesson. B ties it to Sayari's "evidence you can show" promise.
