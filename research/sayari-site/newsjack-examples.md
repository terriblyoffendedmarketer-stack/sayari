> We counted 7 cases in its overview where warnings, checks, or policies were set aside

> The full report is a 26-minute read. But here are headlines from seven cases it details, for you to digest in about a minute:
>
> 1) Nornickel - After the US sanctioned Russian oligarch Oleg Deripaska, an AML officer warned that the branch was breaching its own sanctions policy because Deripaska indirectly held a stake in the bank. Bankers made an exception because his indirect stake was a minority (13%, per bank records), then later changed the policy to allow clients with less than 10% sanctioned ownership. Nornickel itself is not sanctioned.
> 2) Zoomlion's Belarus JV - ICBC Officials saw no sanctions concern at a 3.03% stake held by MAZ, Zoomlion's partner in the JV, who was targeted by EU sanctions, even as they acknowledged the venture made machinery that could be considered dual-use.

Over a year later, officers found public reports of bribery allegations against two owners. ICBC didn't disburse, but instead of announcing the reversal publicly, the bank kept the action quiet.

An AML expert told ICIJ he should have been classified as a politically exposed person. The son was later arrested and sentenced to prison in Kuwait for his involvement in the 1MDB scandal, which included these payments.

**Source:** New York Times investigation (leaked A7 records); FT estimate.

> A government trading company in Kyrgyzstan paid $1M for car floor mats and auto polish. Except the money was actually used for drone parts.
>
> The New York Times just dug into A7, the Moscow firm behind it, and their leaked records read like a how-to guide for dodging sanctions.
>
> Here's the playbook they ran:
>
> 1) A Russian co. needs goods from abroad but can't pay for them directly due to sanctions.
> 2) A7 sends the money often through one of its international shell companies instead.
> 3) Their software reads the real invoice and spits out a fake one.
> 4) The fake keeps the same amount and date. In one deal, $46K of sensitive sensors with military applications were passed off as harmless LED light boxes.
> 5) They pick a stamp from a database of 6,000+ so the fake looks legit.
> 6) Now a shipment bound for Russia looks like a normal purchase for Kyrgyzstan.
> 7) A7 is able to send similar payments from its shell companies to banks in 80+ countries.
>
> And it worked. In that sensor deal, the supplier's bank didn't raise a single compliance issue. The Financial Times estimates that A7 has done nearly $7 billion in business, though A7 itself has suggested far more
>
> The NYT tied 71 shell companies to A7. When Kyrgyzstan shut down some of the network, A7 just moved to shells in the UAE, Bahrain, Nigeria, Mongolia, and a few other places.
>
> Risk teams won't catch this by reading the invoice, because the invoice is FAKE. They catch it in the network. Two of A7's UAE companies were registered by the same guy, a Dubai-based employee of a Russian trucking company.
>
> That's the kind of connection Sayari surfaces. Risk teams use it to map ownership and relationships across borders, so the front company and the network behind it show up in one place.

**Source:** MOFCOM / Ministry of Science and Technology proposal (16 Oct 2026).

> If your company is buying into, investing in, or licensing AI tech from a Chinese company, Beijing may now want to see that deal first.
>
> On October 16, China’s Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) and Ministry of Science and Technology proposed adding 40 technologies to the country’s export-control catalogue, including AI model weights, source code, and AI chip designs.
>
> The list is open for comment until November 15, but the registration requirement already applies.
>
> Until the list is final, Chinese technology exporters must register with MOFCOM before they:
>
> 1. Transfer ownership of one of the 40 listed technologies to a party outside China
> 2. License it for use outside China
> 3. Change its owner or actual controller to a foreign party
> 4. Do anything else MOFCOM specifies later
>
> For foreign buyers and investors, number 3 matters most. An “actual controller” can steer a company through investments or agreements without owning shares. So a deal that gives a foreign party that kind of influence over a Chinese company holding a listed technology can be treated like an export.
>

>
> What that means for your team:
> -Deals involving listed tech may need a MOFCOM registration step before closing.
> -Diligence has to cover who controls the target, including through investments and agreements, along with who owns it.

## Caption skeleton (all four)
1. **Cold open:** the most concrete, surprising detail (floor mats → drone parts; 70 GPU servers; "Beijing may now want to see that deal first").
2. **The news, attributed:** "The NYT just dug into…", "Federal prosecutors in LA have charged…", plus the hedge.
3. **"Here's how it worked":** a numbered 4–7 step breakdown.
4. `--` then one or two extra striking details.
5. `--` then **"the questions for risk teams are" / "what that means for your team"**: 2–3 dashed bullets.
6. **One Sayari line:** what Sayari traces (ownership, network, 250+ jurisdictions), so that X and Y "show up side by side / in one place".
7. Optional closing question (ICBC: "what does your memo cite?").
Words: about 240 (MOFCOM) to 440 (ICBC). No emojis, no hashtags.
